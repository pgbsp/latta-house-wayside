Moving a House

How do you think you would try to move a house 20 miles?

Standing before you is the oldest building preserved at Prairie Grove Battlefield State Park. The Latta House and surrounding structures originally resided in present-day Evansville, Arkansas, about 20 miles southwest of this location. Enslaved people built this house between 1833 and 1834.

John Latta became the postmaster for the area, which he called “The Lord’s Vinyard,” usually shortened to just “Vinyard.” The Latta family abandoned the homestead in 1860 at the beginning of the Civil War. John Latta died in Fort Smith, Arkansas in 1861, and his descendants never returned to live on the property.

Various other families lived at the homestead over the next 100 years until ownership reverted to F.F. Latta, a grandson of John Latta. He donated the buildings on the homestead to Washington County Historical Society in 1957, who decided to move them to the Prairie Grove Battlefield Park.

The workers charged with moving the house faced several problems. The materials used to build the house were over 100 years old – it would be challenging to make sure everything stayed intact. Very carefully, workers labelled every piece of wood and stone in the house and began deconstruction. Once they moved everything to this site, they carefully reassembled the house in its original form.

Today, the Prairie Grove Battlefield State Park remains the steward of this house.

Latta House
Originally constructed in 1834 and relocated to Prairie Grove Battlefield State Park in 1958 for preservation.
Built in 1834, the Latta House stands as one of the oldest structures preserved at Prairie Grove Battlefield State Park. Although associated today with the battlefield, the house tells a broader story of frontier settlement, enslaved labor, and life in northwest Arkansas before the Civil War. Historical records indicate that two enslaved craftsmen, Dan and Ben, performed much of the masonry and woodworking that still survives in the house today. Relocated to the battlefield park in 1958, the structure remains an important reminder of the many people whose labor shaped the Arkansas Ozarks.
